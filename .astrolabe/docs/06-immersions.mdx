## §1 Introduction

In this chapter we consider an isometric immersion $f:M\to\overline{M}$ of a manifold
$M$ of dimension $n$ into a Riemannian manifold $\overline{M}$ of dimension
$k=n+m$. The Riemannian metric of $\overline{M}$ induces, in a natural manner, a
Riemannian metric on $M$: if $v_1,v_2\in T_pM$, define
$\langle v_1,v_2\rangle=\langle df_p(v_1),df_p(v_2)\rangle$. In this situation $f$
becomes an isometric immersion of $M$ into $\overline{M}$, and we study the
relationship between the geometry of $M$ and that of $\overline{M}$.

The motivation comes from the classical case of surfaces $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Restricting to the case where $S$ is the graph
$\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3;z=f(x,y)\}$ of a differentiable function $f$ with
$f(0,0)=0$ and $f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0$ (so that $S$ is tangent to the $x,y$ plane),
the behavior of $S$ near the origin $0\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is strongly influenced by
the quadratic form

$$
\mathit{II}(x,y)=f_{xx}(0)x^2+2f_{xy}(0)xy+f_{yy}(0)y^2
$$

defined in the $x,y$ plane. $\mathit{II}$ is called the *second fundamental form*
of $S$ at the point $0$, and the Gaussian curvature of $S$ at $0$ is given by

$$
K=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2.\qquad(1)
$$

Our first objective is to generalize the notion of the second fundamental form to
$f:M\to\overline{M}$. Since the codimension $m$ can be larger than $1$, the
quadratic form so defined must take values in a vector space of dimension
$m\geq 1$.

The most important relation between the metrics of $M$ and $\overline{M}$ is the
Gauss formula (\entryref{8990d1ff6ab2}), which generalizes (1) and yields the difference
between the curvatures of $M$ and $\overline{M}$ in terms of the second
fundamental form. Since the curvatures are defined intrinsically, the Gauss
formula generalizes the fundamental theorem of Gauss (the point of departure for
Riemannian Geometry). Using the Gauss formula we give a geometric interpretation
of sectional curvature that is essentially the definition used by Riemann
(Cf. Chap. 4, Introduction). In the last section we introduce the equations of
Codazzi and Ricci, which together with Gauss' equation form the fundamental
equations of the local theory of isometric immersions.

## §2 The second fundamental form

Let $f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+m=k}$ be an immersion. For each $p\in M$ there is a
neighborhood $U\subset M$ of $p$ such that $f(U)\subset\overline{M}$ is a
submanifold of $\overline{M}$. To simplify notation we identify $U$ with $f(U)$
and each vector $v\in T_qM$, $q\in U$, with $df_q(v)\in T_{f(q)}\overline{M}$, and
use such identifications to extend a local vector field on $M$ to a local vector
field on $\overline{M}$.

For each $p\in M$ the inner product on $T_p\overline{M}$ splits it into the direct
sum

$$
T_p\overline{M}=T_pM\oplus(T_pM)^\perp,
$$

where $(T_pM)^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $T_pM$ in $T_p\overline{M}$.
If $v\in T_p\overline{M}$ we write $v=v^T+v^N$, $v^T\in T_pM$,
$v^N\in(T_pM)^\perp$, calling $v^T$ the *tangential component* and $v^N$ the
*normal component* of $v$; this splitting is differentiable.

The Riemannian connection of $\overline{M}$ is denoted $\overline{\nabla}$. If
$X,Y$ are local vector fields on $M$ and $\overline{X},\overline{Y}$ local
extensions to $\overline{M}$, define

$$
\nabla_X Y=(\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y})^T;
$$

this is the Riemannian connection relative to the induced metric on $M$
(Cf. Exercise 3 of Chap. 2). To define the second fundamental form, set, for $X,Y$
local vector fields on $M$,

$$
B(X,Y)=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}-\nabla_X Y,
$$

a local vector field on $\overline{M}$ normal to $M$. $B(X,Y)$ does not depend on
the extensions $\overline{X},\overline{Y}$. We denote by $\mathcal{X}(U)^\perp$ the
differentiable vector fields on $U$ that are normal to $f(U)\approx U$.

## §3 The fundamental equations

Given an isometric immersion $f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+m}$, at each $p\in M$ we have
$T_p\overline{M}=T_pM\oplus(T_pM)^\perp$, varying differentiably with $p$. Locally
$T\overline{M}$ over $M$ decomposes into the direct sum of the *tangent bundle*
$TM$ and the *normal bundle* $TM^\perp$. We use Latin letters $X,Y,Z$ for fields
tangent to $M$ and Greek letters $\xi,\eta,\zeta$ for fields normal to $M$.

Given $X$ and $\eta$, the tangent component of $\overline{\nabla}_X\eta$ is
$(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^T=-S_\eta(X)$. The normal component is called the
*normal connection* $\nabla^\perp$ of the immersion:

$$
\nabla_X^\perp\eta=(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^N=\overline{\nabla}_X\eta-(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^T=\overline{\nabla}_X\eta+S_\eta(X).\qquad(5)
$$

The normal connection $\nabla^\perp$ has the usual properties of a connection:
linear in $X$, additive in $\eta$, and
$\nabla_X^\perp(f\eta)=f\nabla_X^\perp\eta+X(f)\eta$, $f\in\mathcal{D}(M)$. As with
the tangent bundle, we introduce the *normal curvature* $R^\perp$ of the immersion,

$$
R^\perp(X,Y)\eta=\nabla_Y^\perp\nabla_X^\perp\eta-\nabla_X^\perp\nabla_Y^\perp\eta+\nabla_{[X,Y]}^\perp\eta.
$$
