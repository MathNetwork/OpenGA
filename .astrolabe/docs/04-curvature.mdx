## §1 Introduction

The notion of curvature in a Riemannian manifold was introduced by Riemann in a
geometric manner. Let $p$ be a point of a Riemannian manifold $M$ and let
$\sigma\subset T_pM$ be a two-dimensional subspace of the tangent space. Consider
the geodesics that start at $p$ and are tangent to $\sigma$; their segments in a
normal neighborhood $U\subset M$ of $p$ determine a two-dimensional submanifold
$S\subset M$ (with the present notation, $S$ is the image of $\exp_p$ restricted to
$\sigma\cap\exp_p^{-1}(U)$). $S$ has a metric induced from the inclusion. Since
Gauss proved that the curvature of a surface can be expressed in terms of its
metric, Riemann could speak of the curvature of $S$ at $p$, denoted $K(p,\sigma)$;
nowadays $K(p,\sigma)$ is called the *sectional curvature* of $M$ at $p$ with
respect to $\sigma$. It is a natural generalization of the Gaussian curvature of
surfaces, and if $M=\mathbb{R}^n$ then $K(p,\sigma)=0$ for all $p$ and all
$\sigma$. A workable formulation of the concept of curvature took a long time to
develop; this chapter presents a definition (Def. 2.1) that intuitively measures
the amount that a Riemannian manifold deviates from being Euclidean. In Chapter 6
it is shown that the notion of sectional curvature (Def. 3.2) obtained from this
definition generalizes the Gaussian curvature for surfaces and coincides with the
concept introduced by Riemann.

## §3 Sectional curvature

Given a vector space $V$, we denote by $|x\wedge y|$ the expression

$$
\sqrt{|x|^2|y|^2-\langle x,y\rangle^2},
$$

which represents the area of a two-dimensional parallelogram determined by the
pair of vectors $x,y\in V$.

## §4 Ricci curvature and scalar curvature

Certain combinations of sectional curvature appear with such frequency that they
deserve special names. Let $x=z_n$ be a unit vector in $T_pM$; we take an
orthonormal basis $\{z_1,z_2,\dots,z_{n-1}\}$ of the hyperplane in $T_pM$
orthogonal to $x$ and consider the following averages:

$$
\mathrm{Ric}_p(x)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i\langle R(x,z_i)x,z_i\rangle,\quad i=1,2,\dots,n-1,
$$

$$
K(p)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j\mathrm{Ric}_p(z_j)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{ij}\langle R(z_i,z_j)z_i,z_j\rangle,\quad j=1,\dots,n.
$$

These expressions do not depend on the choice of the corresponding orthonormal
basis; they are called the *Ricci curvature* in the direction $x$ and the *scalar
curvature* at $p$, respectively. To prove independence, define a bilinear form on
$T_pM$ by

$$
Q(x,y)=\text{trace of the mapping }z\mapsto R(x,z)y.
$$

Choosing $x$ a unit vector and completing it to an orthonormal basis
$\{z_1,\dots,z_{n-1},z_n=x\}$ of $T_pM$ we have

$$
Q(x,y)=\sum_i\langle R(x,z_i)y,z_i\rangle=\sum_i\langle R(y,z_i)x,z_i\rangle=Q(y,x),
$$

that is, $Q$ is symmetric and $Q(x,x)=(n-1)\mathrm{Ric}_p(x)$; this proves that
$\mathrm{Ric}_p(x)$ is intrinsically defined. The bilinear form $Q$ corresponds to
a linear self-adjoint mapping $K$ given by $\langle K(x),y\rangle=Q(x,y)$, and
taking an orthonormal basis $\{z_1,\dots,z_n\}$,

$$
\text{Trace of }K=\sum_j\langle K(z_j),z_j\rangle=\sum_j Q(z_j,z_j)=(n-1)\sum_j\mathrm{Ric}_p(z_j)=n(n-1)K(p),
$$

which proves the statement. The bilinear form $\frac{1}{n-1}Q$ is, at times,
called *the Ricci tensor*. In a coordinate system $(x_i)$ with
$X_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}$, $g_{ij}=\langle X_i,X_j\rangle$ and $g^{ij}$
the inverse matrix of $g_{ij}$, the coefficients of the bilinear form
$\frac{1}{n-1}Q$ in the basis $\{X_i\}$ are

$$
\frac{1}{n-1}R_{ik}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_j R_{ijk}^j=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{sj}R_{ijks}g^{sj},
$$

and the scalar curvature is given by

$$
K=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{ik}R_{ik}g^{ik}.
$$

## §5 Tensors on Riemannian manifolds

The notion of curvature is a particular case of the idea of a tensor. The idea of
a tensor is a natural generalization of the idea of a vector field, an important
point being that, analogously to vector fields, tensors can be differentiated
covariantly. For what follows it is useful to observe that $\mathcal{X}(M)$ is a
module over $\mathcal{D}(M)$.
