## §1 Introduction

The notion of differentiable manifold extends the methods of differential
calculus to spaces more general than $\mathbb{R}^n$. The motivating example is a
*regular surface* $S\subset\mathbb{R}^3$: a subset such that for every $p\in S$
there is a neighborhood $V$ of $p$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and a map
$\mathbf{x}:U\subset\mathbb{R}^2\to V\cap S$ of an open set $U$ onto $V\cap S$
with (a) $\mathbf{x}$ a differentiable homeomorphism and (b)
$(d\mathbf{x})_q:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3$ injective for all $q\in U$ (cf.
do Carmo [dC 2], Chap. 2). The key consequence is that the transition between
two parametrizations $\mathbf{x}_\alpha,\mathbf{x}_\beta$ with
$\mathbf{x}_\alpha(U_\alpha)\cap\mathbf{x}_\beta(U_\beta)=W\neq\phi$ is a
diffeomorphism: $\mathbf{x}_\beta^{-1}\circ\mathbf{x}_\alpha$ and
$\mathbf{x}_\alpha^{-1}\circ\mathbf{x}_\beta$ are differentiable (cf. \entryref{2a37e4843dff}
below). The major defect of this definition is its dependence on $\mathbb{R}^3$.
The abstract definition (independent of the ambient space) is presented in the
next section for arbitrary dimension $n$. Throughout, *differentiable* always
signifies of class $C^\infty$.
