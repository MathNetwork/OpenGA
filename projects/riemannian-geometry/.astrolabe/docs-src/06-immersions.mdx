## §1 Introduction

In this chapter we consider an isometric immersion $f:M\to\overline{M}$ of a manifold
$M$ of dimension $n$ into a Riemannian manifold $\overline{M}$ of dimension
$k=n+m$. The Riemannian metric of $\overline{M}$ induces, in a natural manner, a
Riemannian metric on $M$: if $v_1,v_2\in T_pM$, define
$\langle v_1,v_2\rangle=\langle df_p(v_1),df_p(v_2)\rangle$. In this situation $f$
becomes an isometric immersion of $M$ into $\overline{M}$, and we study the
relationship between the geometry of $M$ and that of $\overline{M}$.

The motivation comes from the classical case of surfaces $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Restricting to the case where $S$ is the graph
$\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3;z=f(x,y)\}$ of a differentiable function $f$ with
$f(0,0)=0$ and $f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0$ (so that $S$ is tangent to the $x,y$ plane),
the behavior of $S$ near the origin $0\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is strongly influenced by
the quadratic form

$$
\mathit{II}(x,y)=f_{xx}(0)x^2+2f_{xy}(0)xy+f_{yy}(0)y^2
$$

defined in the $x,y$ plane. $\mathit{II}$ is called the *second fundamental form*
of $S$ at the point $0$, and the Gaussian curvature of $S$ at $0$ is given by

$$
K=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2.\qquad(1)
$$

Our first objective is to generalize the notion of the second fundamental form to
$f:M\to\overline{M}$. Since the codimension $m$ can be larger than $1$, the
quadratic form so defined must take values in a vector space of dimension
$m\geq 1$.

The most important relation between the metrics of $M$ and $\overline{M}$ is the
Gauss formula (Theorem 2.5), which generalizes (1) and yields the difference
between the curvatures of $M$ and $\overline{M}$ in terms of the second
fundamental form. Since the curvatures are defined intrinsically, the Gauss
formula generalizes the fundamental theorem of Gauss (the point of departure for
Riemannian Geometry). Using the Gauss formula we give a geometric interpretation
of sectional curvature that is essentially the definition used by Riemann
(Cf. Chap. 4, Introduction). In the last section we introduce the equations of
Codazzi and Ricci, which together with Gauss' equation form the fundamental
equations of the local theory of isometric immersions.

## §2 The second fundamental form

Let $f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+m=k}$ be an immersion. For each $p\in M$ there is a
neighborhood $U\subset M$ of $p$ such that $f(U)\subset\overline{M}$ is a
submanifold of $\overline{M}$. To simplify notation we identify $U$ with $f(U)$
and each vector $v\in T_qM$, $q\in U$, with $df_q(v)\in T_{f(q)}\overline{M}$, and
use such identifications to extend a local vector field on $M$ to a local vector
field on $\overline{M}$.

For each $p\in M$ the inner product on $T_p\overline{M}$ splits it into the direct
sum

$$
T_p\overline{M}=T_pM\oplus(T_pM)^\perp,
$$

where $(T_pM)^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $T_pM$ in $T_p\overline{M}$.
If $v\in T_p\overline{M}$ we write $v=v^T+v^N$, $v^T\in T_pM$,
$v^N\in(T_pM)^\perp$, calling $v^T$ the *tangential component* and $v^N$ the
*normal component* of $v$; this splitting is differentiable.

The Riemannian connection of $\overline{M}$ is denoted $\overline{\nabla}$. If
$X,Y$ are local vector fields on $M$ and $\overline{X},\overline{Y}$ local
extensions to $\overline{M}$, define

$$
\nabla_X Y=(\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y})^T;
$$

this is the Riemannian connection relative to the induced metric on $M$
(Cf. Exercise 3 of Chap. 2). To define the second fundamental form, set, for $X,Y$
local vector fields on $M$,

$$
B(X,Y)=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}-\nabla_X Y,
$$

a local vector field on $\overline{M}$ normal to $M$. $B(X,Y)$ does not depend on
the extensions $\overline{X},\overline{Y}$. We denote by $\mathcal{X}(U)^\perp$ the
differentiable vector fields on $U$ that are normal to $f(U)\approx U$.

### 2.1 Proposition (the second fundamental form mapping $B$)
If $X,Y\in\mathcal{X}(U)$, the mapping
$B:\mathcal{X}(U)\times\mathcal{X}(U)\to\mathcal{X}(U)^\perp$ given by

$$
B(X,Y)=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}-\nabla_X Y
$$

is bilinear and symmetric.

*Proof.* From linearity of a connection, $B$ is additive in $X$ and $Y$ and
$B(fX,Y)=fB(X,Y)$, $f\in\mathcal{D}(U)$. To show $B(X,fY)=fB(X,Y)$, denote the
extension of $f$ to $\overline{U}$ by $\overline{f}$; then

$$
B(X,fY)=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}(\overline{f}\,\overline{Y})-\nabla_X(fY)=\overline{f}\,\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}-f\nabla_X Y+\overline{X}(\overline{f})\overline{Y}-X(f)Y.
$$

Since $f=\overline{f}$, $\overline{X}(\overline{f})=X(f)$, and $Y=\overline{Y}$ on
$M$, the last two terms cancel, giving $B(X,fY)=fB(X,Y)$. To show $B$ is
symmetric, by symmetry of the Riemannian connection,

$$
B(X,Y)=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}-\nabla_X Y=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{X}+[\overline{X},\overline{Y}]-\nabla_Y X-[X,Y].
$$

Since $[\overline{X},\overline{Y}]=[X,Y]$ on $M$, we conclude $B(X,Y)=B(Y,X)$.
$\square$

Because $B$ is bilinear, the value $B(X,Y)(p)$ depends only on the values $X(p)$
and $Y(p)$. Now let $p\in M$ and $\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$. The mapping
$H_\eta:T_pM\times T_pM\to\mathbb{R}$ given by

$$
H_\eta(x,y)=\langle B(x,y),\eta\rangle,\quad x,y\in T_pM,
$$

is, by Proposition 2.1, a symmetric bilinear form.

### 2.2 Definition (second fundamental form $\mathit{II}_\eta$)
The quadratic form $\mathit{II}_\eta$ defined on $T_pM$ by

$$
\mathit{II}_\eta(x)=H_\eta(x,x)
$$

is called *the second fundamental form* of $f$ at $p$ along the normal vector
$\eta$. Sometimes the expression *second fundamental form* also designates the
mapping $B$. The bilinear mapping $H_\eta$ is associated to a linear self-adjoint
operator $S_\eta:T_pM\to T_pM$ by

$$
\langle S_\eta(x),y\rangle=H_\eta(x,y)=\langle B(x,y),\eta\rangle.
$$

### 2.3 Proposition (the operator $S_\eta$ via the covariant derivative)
Let $p\in M$, $x\in T_pM$ and $\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$. Let $N$ be a local extension
of $\eta$ normal to $M$. Then

$$
S_\eta(x)=-(\overline{\nabla}_x N)^T.
$$

*Proof.* Let $y\in T_pM$ and let $X,Y$ be local extensions of $x,y$, tangent to
$M$. Then $\langle N,Y\rangle=0$, and therefore

$$
\langle S_\eta(x),y\rangle=\langle B(X,Y)(p),N\rangle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_X Y-\nabla_X Y,N\rangle(p)=\langle\overline{\nabla}_X Y,N\rangle(p)=-\langle Y,\overline{\nabla}_X N\rangle(p)=\langle-\overline{\nabla}_x N,y\rangle,
$$

for all $y\in T_pM$. $\square$

### 2.4 Example (hypersurface; principal curvatures; Gauss spherical mapping)
Consider the case in which the codimension of the immersion is $1$, i.e.
$f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+1}$; $f(M)\subset\overline{M}$ is then called a
*hypersurface*. Let $p\in M$ and $\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$, $|\eta|=1$. Since
$S_\eta:T_pM\to T_pM$ is symmetric, there exists an orthonormal basis of
eigenvectors $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ of $T_pM$ with real eigenvalues
$\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$, $S_\eta(e_i)=\lambda_i e_i$. If $M$ and $\overline{M}$
are both orientable and oriented, then $\eta$ is uniquely determined by requiring
$\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ to be in the orientation of $M$ and $\{e_1,\dots,e_n,\eta\}$ in
the orientation of $\overline{M}$. The $e_i$ are *principal directions* and the
$\lambda_i=k_i$ are *principal curvatures* of $f$. The symmetric functions of the
$\lambda_i$ are invariants of the immersion: $\det(S_\eta)=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$
is the *Gauss-Kronecker curvature* and
$\frac{1}{n}(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n)$ the *mean curvature* of $f$.

When $\overline{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, take $N$ a local unit normal extension of
$\eta$. Let $S_1^n=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1};|x|=1\}$ and define the *Gauss spherical
mapping* $g:M^n\to S_1^n$ by translating the origin of $N$ to the origin of
$\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, with $g(q)=$ endpoint of the translation of $N(q)$. Since
$T_qM$ and $T_{g(q)}S_1^n$ are parallel, we identify them and find

$$
dg_q(x)=\frac{d}{dt}(N\circ c(t))_{t=0}=\overline{\nabla}_x N=(\overline{\nabla}_x N)^T=-S_\eta(x),
$$

where $c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M$ has $c(0)=q$, $c'(0)=x$, using
$\langle N,N\rangle=1$. Thus $-S_\eta$ is the derivative of the Gauss spherical
mapping. As an application: if $M^n$, $n\geq 2$, is a connected, compact,
orientable manifold and there exists an immersion $f:M^n\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with
non-vanishing Gauss-Kronecker curvature at every point, then $M$ is diffeomorphic
to $S_1^n$ (the proof depends on covering spaces; $\det(dg_p)\neq 0$ makes
$g:M\to S_1^n$ a local diffeomorphism, hence a covering map since $M$ is compact,
hence a diffeomorphism since $S_1^n$ is simply connected).

### 2.5 Theorem (Gauss)
Let $p\in M$ and let $x,y$ be orthonormal vectors in $T_pM$. Then

$$
K(x,y)-\overline{K}(x,y)=\langle B(x,x),B(y,y)\rangle-|B(x,y)|^2,\qquad(1)
$$

where $K(x,y)$ and $\overline{K}(x,y)$ denote the sectional curvatures of $M$ and
$\overline{M}$, respectively, in the plane generated by $x$ and $y$.

*Proof.* Let $X,Y$ be local orthogonal extensions of $x,y$, tangent to $M$, with
extensions $\overline{X},\overline{Y}$ to $\overline{M}$. Then

$$
K(x,y)-\overline{K}(x,y)=\langle\nabla_Y\nabla_X X-\nabla_X\nabla_Y X-(\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{X}-\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{X}),Y\rangle(p)+\langle\nabla_{[X,Y]}X-\overline{\nabla}_{[\overline{X},\overline{Y}]}\overline{X},Y\rangle(p).
$$

The last term is zero because
$\langle\nabla_{[X,Y]}X-\overline{\nabla}_{[\overline{X},\overline{Y}]}\overline{X},Y\rangle(p)=-\langle(\overline{\nabla}_{[\overline{X},\overline{Y}]}\overline{X})^N,Y\rangle(p)=0$.
Denoting by $E_1,\dots,E_m$, $m=\dim\overline{M}-\dim M$, local orthonormal fields
normal to $M$, we have $B(X,Y)=\sum_i H_i(X,Y)E_i$, $H_i=H_{E_i}$. Then at $p$,

$$
\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{X}=\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\Big(\sum_i H_i(X,X)E_i+\nabla_X X\Big)=\sum_i\{H_i(X,X)\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}E_i+\overline{Y}H_i(X,X)E_i\}+\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\nabla_X X,
$$

hence

$$
\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{X},Y\rangle=-\sum_i H_i(X,X)H_i(Y,Y)+\langle\nabla_Y\nabla_X X,Y\rangle,\qquad(2)
$$

and similarly

$$
\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{X}}\overline{\nabla}_{\overline{Y}}\overline{X},Y\rangle=-\sum_i H_i(X,Y)H_i(X,Y)+\langle\nabla_X\nabla_Y X,Y\rangle.\qquad(3)
$$

Using (2) and (3) we obtain (1). $\square$

### 2.6 Remark (Gauss formula for a hypersurface)
In the case of a hypersurface $f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+1}$, let $p\in M$,
$\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$, $|\eta|=1$, and $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ an orthonormal basis of $T_pM$ in
which $S_\eta=S$ is diagonal, $S(e_i)=\lambda_ie_i$. Then $H(e_i,e_i)=\lambda_i$
and $H(e_i,e_j)=0$ if $i\neq j$, so (1) becomes

$$
K(e_i,e_j)-\overline{K}(e_i,e_j)=\lambda_i\lambda_j.\qquad(4)
$$

### 2.7 Remark (Theorema Egregium)
In the case $M=M^2\subset\overline{M}=\mathbb{R}^3$, the product $\lambda_1\lambda_2$
of the principal curvatures coincides with the Gaussian curvature of the surface.
Remark 2.6 then shows that the Gaussian curvature coincides with the sectional
curvature of the surface, and implies the famous *Theorema Egregium* of Gauss:
the Gaussian curvature of $M^2\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ is an invariant under
isometries.

### 2.8 Example (curvature of $S^n$)
The sectional curvature of the unit sphere $S^n\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is constant
equal to $1$. Orient $S^n$ by the inward pointing unit normal $N(x)=-x$,
$|x|=1$. The Gauss mapping is then equal to $(-i)$, $i$ the identity of $S^n$, so
the self-adjoint operator associated to $H_N$ has all eigenvalues equal to $1$.
Thus every $v\in T_pS^n$ is an eigenvector; by (4) all sectional curvatures of
$S^n$ equal $1$.

An immersion $f:M\to\overline{M}$ is *geodesic at* $p\in M$ if for every
$\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$ the second fundamental form $H_\eta$ is identically zero at
$p$. An immersion $f$ is *totally geodesic* if it is geodesic for all $p\in M$.

### 2.9 Proposition (totally geodesic criterion)
An immersion $f:M\to\overline{M}$ is geodesic at $p\in M$ if and only if every
geodesic $\gamma$ of $M$ starting from $p$ is a geodesic of $\overline{M}$ at $p$.

*Proof.* Let $\gamma(0)=p$, $\gamma'(0)=x$. Let $N$ be a local extension, normal to
$M$, of a vector $\eta$ normal to $M$ at $p$, and $X$ a local extension of
$\gamma'(t)$ to a tangent field on $M$. Since $\langle X,N\rangle=0$, at $p$,

$$
H_\eta(x,x)=\langle S_\eta(x),x\rangle=-\langle\overline{\nabla}_X N,X\rangle=-X\langle N,X\rangle+\langle N,\overline{\nabla}_X X\rangle=\langle N,\overline{\nabla}_X X\rangle.
$$

Thus $f$ is geodesic at $p$ iff for all $x\in T_pM$ the geodesic $\gamma$ tangent
to $x$ at $p$ satisfies: $\overline{\nabla}_X X(p)$ has no normal component;
equivalently, every geodesic $\gamma$ of $M$ starting from $p$ is a geodesic of
$\overline{M}$ at $p$. $\square$

Proposition 2.9 gives the geometric interpretation of sectional curvature. Let
$B\subset T_pM$ be an open ball on which $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism, and
$\sigma\subset T_pM$ a two-dimensional subspace. Then $\exp_p(\sigma\cap B)=S$ is a
two-dimensional submanifold of $M$ through $p$, the surface formed by "small"
geodesics tangent to $\sigma$ at $p$. By Proposition 2.9, $S$ is geodesic at $p$,
so the second fundamental forms of $i:S\subset M$ vanish at $p$. Denoting by $K_S$
the Gaussian curvature of $S$ at $p$, the Gauss formula gives

$$
K_S(p)=K(p,\sigma).
$$

This is exactly the way Riemann defined sectional curvature in [Ri]. It was proven
by E. Cartan that if a Riemannian manifold $M$ has, for every $p$ and every
two-dimensional $\sigma\subset T_pM$, a totally geodesic submanifold tangent to
$\sigma$, then $M$ has constant sectional curvature.

### 2.10 Definition (minimal immersion; mean curvature vector)
An immersion $f:M\to\overline{M}$ is called *minimal* if for every $p\in M$ and
every $\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$ the trace of $S_\eta$ is zero. Choosing an orthonormal frame
$E_1,\dots,E_m$ of vectors in $\mathcal{X}(U)^\perp$, where $U$ is a neighborhood
of $p$ in which $f$ is an embedding, we write at $p$

$$
B(x,y)=\sum_i H_i(x,y)E_i,\quad x,y\in T_pM,\ i=1,\dots,m,
$$

with $H_i=H_{E_i}$. The normal vector

$$
H=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(\operatorname{trace}S_i)E_i,
$$

where $S_i=S_{E_i}$, does not depend on the chosen frame; it is the *mean
curvature vector* of $f$. Thus $f$ is minimal iff $H(p)=0$ for all $p\in M$. Such
immersions minimize volume in the induced metric, in the same way that geodesics
minimize arc length.

## §3 The fundamental equations

Given an isometric immersion $f:M^n\to\overline{M}^{n+m}$, at each $p\in M$ we have
$T_p\overline{M}=T_pM\oplus(T_pM)^\perp$, varying differentiably with $p$. Locally
$T\overline{M}$ over $M$ decomposes into the direct sum of the *tangent bundle*
$TM$ and the *normal bundle* $TM^\perp$. We use Latin letters $X,Y,Z$ for fields
tangent to $M$ and Greek letters $\xi,\eta,\zeta$ for fields normal to $M$.

Given $X$ and $\eta$, the tangent component of $\overline{\nabla}_X\eta$ is
$(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^T=-S_\eta(X)$. The normal component is called the
*normal connection* $\nabla^\perp$ of the immersion:

$$
\nabla_X^\perp\eta=(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^N=\overline{\nabla}_X\eta-(\overline{\nabla}_X\eta)^T=\overline{\nabla}_X\eta+S_\eta(X).\qquad(5)
$$

The normal connection $\nabla^\perp$ has the usual properties of a connection:
linear in $X$, additive in $\eta$, and
$\nabla_X^\perp(f\eta)=f\nabla_X^\perp\eta+X(f)\eta$, $f\in\mathcal{D}(M)$. As with
the tangent bundle, we introduce the *normal curvature* $R^\perp$ of the immersion,

$$
R^\perp(X,Y)\eta=\nabla_Y^\perp\nabla_X^\perp\eta-\nabla_X^\perp\nabla_Y^\perp\eta+\nabla_{[X,Y]}^\perp\eta.
$$

### 3.1 Proposition (Gauss equation and Ricci equation)
The following equations are valid.

(a) *Gauss equation*

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)Z,T\rangle=\langle R(X,Y)Z,T\rangle-\langle B(Y,T),B(X,Z)\rangle+\langle B(X,T),B(Y,Z)\rangle.
$$

(b) *Ricci equation*

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle-\langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle=\langle[S_\eta,S_\zeta]X,Y\rangle,
$$

where $[S_\eta,S_\zeta]$ denotes the operator $S_\eta\circ S_\zeta-S_\zeta\circ S_\eta$.

*Proof.* Observe that $\overline{\nabla}_X Y=\nabla_X Y+B(X,Y)$. Since

$$
\overline{R}(X,Y)Z=\overline{\nabla}_Y\overline{\nabla}_X Z-\overline{\nabla}_X\overline{\nabla}_Y Z+\overline{\nabla}_{[X,Y]}Z=\overline{\nabla}_Y(\nabla_X Z+B(X,Z))-\overline{\nabla}_X(\nabla_Y Z+B(Y,Z))+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z+B([X,Y],Z),
$$

we have

$$
\overline{R}(X,Y)Z=R(X,Y)Z+B(Y,\nabla_X Z)+\nabla_Y^\perp B(X,Z)-S_{B(X,Z)}Y-B(X,\nabla_Y Z)-\nabla_X^\perp B(Y,Z)+S_{B(Y,Z)}X+B([X,Y],Z).\qquad(6)
$$

Taking the inner product of (6) with $T$, the normal terms vanish and

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)Z,T\rangle=\langle R(X,Y)Z,T\rangle-\langle S_{B(X,Z)}Y,T\rangle+\langle S_{B(Y,Z)}X,T\rangle=\langle R(X,Y)Z,T\rangle-\langle B(Y,T),B(X,Z)\rangle+\langle B(X,T),B(Y,Z)\rangle,
$$

which is the Gauss equation. For the Ricci equation, calculate

$$
\overline{R}(X,Y)\eta=\overline{\nabla}_Y\overline{\nabla}_X\eta-\overline{\nabla}_X\overline{\nabla}_Y\eta+\overline{\nabla}_{[X,Y]}\eta=R^\perp(X,Y)\eta-S_{\nabla_X^\perp\eta}Y-\nabla_Y(S_\eta X)-B(S_\eta X,Y)+S_{\nabla_Y^\perp\eta}X+\nabla_X(S_\eta Y)+B(X,S_\eta Y)-S_\eta[X,Y].
$$

Multiplying by $\zeta$ and observing that $\langle B(X,Y),\eta\rangle=\langle S_\eta X,Y\rangle$,

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle=\langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle-\langle B(S_\eta X,Y),\zeta\rangle+\langle B(X,S_\eta Y),\zeta\rangle=\langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle+\langle(S_\eta S_\zeta-S_\zeta S_\eta)X,Y\rangle=\langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle+\langle[S_\eta,S_\zeta]X,Y\rangle,
$$

which is Ricci's equation. $\square$

### 3.2 Remark
Theorem 2.5 of this chapter is a special case of Gauss' equation.

### 3.3 Remark (flat normal bundle)
We say the normal bundle of an immersion is *flat* if $R^\perp=0$. Assume the
ambient space $\overline{M}$ has constant sectional curvature. Then the Ricci
equation can be written

$$
\langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle=-\langle[S_\eta,S_\zeta]X,Y\rangle.
$$

It follows that $R^\perp=0$ if and only if $[S_\eta,S_\zeta]=0$ for all
$\eta,\zeta$, that is, if and only if for all $p\in M$ there exists a basis of
$T_pM$ which diagonalizes all of the $S_\eta$ simultaneously.

The Gauss and Ricci equations are algebraic relations between the curvatures of
the tangent and normal bundles and the second fundamental form. A non-algebraic
relation is the Codazzi equation, for which we differentiate the second
fundamental form considered as a tensor
$B:\mathcal{X}(M)\times\mathcal{X}(M)\times\mathcal{X}(M)^\perp\to\mathbb{R}$,
$B(X,Y,\eta)=\langle B(X,Y),\eta\rangle$, with covariant derivative

$$
(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta)=X(B(Y,Z,\eta))-B(\nabla_X Y,Z,\eta)-B(Y,\nabla_X Z,\eta)-B(Y,Z,\nabla_X^\perp\eta).
$$

### 3.4 Proposition (Codazzi's equation)
With the notation above,

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)Z,\eta\rangle=(\overline{\nabla}_Y B)(X,Z,\eta)-(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta).
$$

*Proof.* Observe first that

$$
(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta)=X\langle B(Y,Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(\nabla_X Y,Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(Y,\nabla_X Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(Y,Z),\nabla_X^\perp\eta\rangle=\langle\nabla_X^\perp(B(Y,Z)),\eta\rangle-\langle B(\nabla_X Y,Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(Y,\nabla_X Z),\eta\rangle.
$$

Consider expression (6) in the proof of Proposition 3.1 and multiply both sides by
$\eta$. Taking the remark above into account,

$$
\langle\overline{R}(X,Y)Z,\eta\rangle=\langle B(Y,\nabla_X Z),\eta\rangle+\langle\nabla_Y^\perp B(X,Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(X,\nabla_Y Z),\eta\rangle-\langle\nabla_X^\perp B(Y,Z),\eta\rangle+\langle B(\nabla_X Y,Z),\eta\rangle-\langle B(\nabla_Y X,Z),\eta\rangle=-(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta)+(\overline{\nabla}_Y B)(X,Z,\eta),
$$

which is Codazzi's equation. $\square$

### 3.5 Remark (Codazzi for constant curvature ambient and hypersurfaces)
If the ambient space $\overline{M}$ has constant sectional curvature, the Codazzi
equation reduces to

$$
(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta)=(\overline{\nabla}_Y B)(X,Z,\eta).
$$

If, in addition, the codimension of the immersion is $1$ and $\eta$ is chosen as a
local unit normal field, then
$\nabla_X^\perp\eta=0$, hence

$$
(\overline{\nabla}_X B)(Y,Z,\eta)=X\langle S_\eta(Y),Z\rangle-\langle S_\eta(\nabla_X Y),Z\rangle-\langle S_\eta(Y),\nabla_X Z\rangle=\langle\nabla_X(S_\eta(Y)),Z\rangle-\langle S_\eta(\nabla_X Y),Z\rangle.
$$

Therefore, in this case, the Codazzi equation can be written

$$
\nabla_X(S_\eta(Y))-\nabla_Y(S_\eta(X))=S_\eta([X,Y]).
$$

The importance of the equations of Gauss, Codazzi, and Ricci is that, when the
ambient space has constant sectional curvature, they play an analogous role to the
compatibility equations in the local theory of surfaces (Cf. M. do Carmo [dC 2],
pp. 235–236). It is possible to state an analogous theorem to the fundamental
theorem of local surface theory; see K. Tenenblat, "On isometric immersions of
Riemannian manifolds", Boletim da Soc. Bras. de Mat. vol. 2 (1971), 23–36, and
H. Jacobowitz, "The Gauss-Codazzi equations", Tensor N.S., 39 (1982), 15–22.
