## §1 Introduction

After fixing the basic terminology, we pass to the study of two fundamental
concepts of Riemannian geometry: *geodesics* and *curvature*. This chapter
introduces the notion of a geodesic as a curve with zero acceleration; the next
chapter initiates the study of curvature.

One objective of this chapter is to show that a geodesic minimizes arc length
for points "sufficiently close" (in a sense made precise below); conversely, if
a curve minimizes arc length between any two of its points, it is a geodesic.

In §2 we introduce the tangent bundle $TM$ of a differentiable manifold $M$,
which lets us reduce the local study of geodesics on $M$ to the study of the
trajectories of a vector field — the *geodesic field* — on $TM$. We then
introduce the *exponential map* of an open set in $TM$ to $M$, a way of
"collecting" all the geodesics of $M$ into a single differentiable map. This
permits, for example, an application of the inverse function theorem to show that
any point of $M$ possesses a neighborhood $W$ such that any two points of $W$ can
be joined by a unique geodesic that minimizes arc length (see \entryref{f29bd1e6ebb3}).

The concept of a geodesic as a curve minimizing distance between two nearby
points is old. For surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^3$, geodesics are the curves $c(s)$
($s$ arc length) whose acceleration $c''(s)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is perpendicular
to the surface (so the acceleration "from the viewpoint of the surface" is zero);
this was apparently known for convex surfaces to Johann Bernoulli (1697), and the
equations of geodesics for surfaces $f(x,y,z)=0$ were considered by Euler (1732).
It was only with the work of Gauss [Ga] (1827) that the relationship between
geodesics and the curvature of a surface was established (cf. Introduction to
Chap. 1); this relationship is fundamental and reappears throughout the book.

### Tangent bundle (after 2.1)
$TM$ is the set of pairs $(q,v)$, $q\in M$, $v\in T_qM$. If $(U,\mathbf{x})$ is a
coordinate system on $M$, any $v\in T_qM$, $q\in\mathbf{x}(U)$, is
$\sum_i y_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}$, and $(x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ are
coordinates of $(q,v)$ on $TU$, giving $TM$ a differentiable structure (cf.
\entryref{5c7d4e32a0d7} of Chap. 0). Locally $TU=U\times\mathbb{R}^n$, and the canonical
projection $\pi:TM\to M$, $\pi(q,v)=q$, is differentiable. A curve $t\mapsto\gamma(t)$
in $M$ determines $t\mapsto(\gamma(t),\frac{d\gamma}{dt}(t))$ in $TM$; if $\gamma$
is a geodesic, this curve satisfies the first-order system

$$
\begin{cases}\dfrac{dx_k}{dt}=y_k,\\[4pt]\dfrac{dy_k}{dt}=-\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}^k y_i y_j,\end{cases}\qquad k=1,\dots,n,\qquad\text{(1')}
$$

on $TU$. Thus the second-order system (1) on $U$ is equivalent to the first-order
system (1') on $TU$.

### Exponential map (after 2.8)
\entryref{6b083d8aac49} lets us define the *exponential map*. Let $p\in M$ and let
$\mathcal{U}\subset TM$ be as in \entryref{6b083d8aac49}. The map $\exp:\mathcal{U}\to M$,

$$
\exp(q,v)=\gamma(1,q,v)=\gamma\Big(|v|,q,\frac{v}{|v|}\Big),\quad(q,v)\in\mathcal{U},
$$

is the *exponential map* on $\mathcal{U}$; it is differentiable. In applications
we use the restriction to an open set of $T_qM$:

$$
\exp_q:B_\varepsilon(0)\subset T_qM\to M,\qquad\exp_q(v)=\exp(q,v),
$$

where $B_\varepsilon(0)$ is the open ball of center $0$ and radius $\varepsilon$
in $T_qM$. Then $\exp_q$ is differentiable and $\exp_q(0)=q$. Geometrically,
$\exp_q(v)$ is obtained by going a length $|v|$ from $q$ along the geodesic with
velocity $\frac{v}{|v|}$.

### Normal neighborhoods (after \entryref{fa0c59ada60c})
If $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism of a neighborhood $V\ni 0$ in $T_pM$ onto
$U=\exp_p(V)$, then $U$ is a *normal neighborhood* of $p$. If
$\overline{B_\varepsilon(0)}\subset V$, then $\exp_p B_\varepsilon(0)=B_\varepsilon(p)$
is the *normal ball* (or *geodesic ball*) of center $p$ and radius $\varepsilon$.
By the Gauss lemma (3.5), its boundary is a hypersurface orthogonal to the
geodesics from $p$, the *geodesic sphere* $S_\varepsilon(p)$; the geodesics in
$B_\varepsilon(p)$ that begin at $p$ are the *radial geodesics*.

## §4 Convex neighborhoods

By \entryref{f29bd1e6ebb3} (cf. \entryref{ae1f6b0745bc}) every $p\in M$ has a totally normal neighborhood
$W$ with $\delta>0$ so that any two $q_1,q_2\in W$ are joined by a minimizing
geodesic of length $<\delta$; but such a geodesic need not lie entirely in $W$. A
subset $S\subset M$ is *strongly convex* if for any two points $q_1,q_2$ in the
closure $\overline{S}$ there is a unique minimizing geodesic $\gamma$ joining them
whose interior lies in $S$. We show the radius of a totally normal ball can be
chosen so the ball is strongly convex.
